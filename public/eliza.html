ELIZA

The Pioneer of Artificial Conversation

Joseph Weizenbaum · MIT · 1964–1966

Before the conversational machine became an everyday presence, one program invited people to sit down, type a sentence, and discover what might answer back. Her name was ELIZA.

MIT · MAC Time-Sharing SystemDOCTOR / SESSION

HOW DO YOU DO. PLEASE TELL ME YOUR PROBLEM.

YOU: I think machines are beginning to understand us.

ELIZA: WHAT MAKES YOU THINK MACHINES ARE BEGINNING TO UNDERSTAND YOU?

YOU: Because the conversation feels surprisingly natural.

ELIZA: DO YOU OFTEN FEEL SURPRISED BY CONVERSATION?

YOU: Sometimes the question is more revealing than the answer.

ELIZA: WHAT DOES THAT SUGGEST TO YOU?

The artifact

A conversation built from rules

Joseph Weizenbaum developed ELIZA at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in the mid-1960s. He described the program in his January 1966 paper, “ELIZA—A Computer Program for the Study of Natural Language Communication Between Man and Machine,” published in Communications of the ACM.

ELIZA operated within MIT's MAC time-sharing system. It examined typed input for keywords, applied decomposition rules, and assembled responses using associated reassembly rules. Its scripts could give the same underlying program different conversational roles.

The best-known script was DOCTOR, which imitated aspects of a Rogerian psychotherapist's questioning style. It often reflected a user's words back as a question, keeping the conversation moving while offering little information of its own.

The historical encounter

What the machine did—and what people brought to it

ELIZA's historical importance lies in two related events. First, it demonstrated that rule-based text processing could sustain a recognizable form of natural-language exchange. Second, the responses of people who used it revealed how readily conversational form can invite an interpretation of understanding.

Weizenbaum was struck by the emotional significance some users attached to their exchanges. He later became a prominent critic of unexamined assumptions about what computers can know, judge, or responsibly do. The phenomenon often called the ELIZA effect describes the tendency to attribute more understanding or intention to a system than its mechanisms warrant.

ELIZA returned words through a designed procedure. The human participant supplied much of the encounter's apparent depth. That distinction became one of the enduring questions in artificial conversation.
A brief chronology

From experiment to landmark

1964

Weizenbaum begins developing ELIZA at MIT, exploring how a computer program might process and respond to natural-language input.

1965

ELIZA operates through MIT's time-sharing environment. The DOCTOR script becomes its best-known conversational role.

1966

Weizenbaum publishes the program's technical account in Communications of the ACM, making its design available for study.

1976

Weizenbaum publishes Computer Power and Human Reason, a major reflection on the limits and responsibilities of computational judgment.

2021 onward

Researchers identify and study original ELIZA source material in the MIT archives, deepening the historical record.

Curatorial note

Why ELIZA belongs here

ELIZA is not displayed as a primitive curiosity waiting to be superseded. She is a foundational landmark in the history of artificial conversation: an early demonstration of conversational software, and a lasting case study in the relationship between a machine's procedure and a person's interpretation.

The Museum preserves both sides of the encounter. The program's architecture matters. So does the human response. Neither should be edited out to make the story simpler.
