## 2. Current adopted formulation

> **SYNERGY is the graduated spectrum of consequences arising from the interaction of identities or elements. Interaction necessarily entails Synergy; its consequences may range from generative to destructive.**

The page explicitly corrects the former productive-surplus definition. Greater-than-additive output is one possible generative consequence, not the condition for Synergy to exist.

The adopted architecture distinguishes:
- **Interaction:** the relational occurrence/process in which identities or elements affect or respond to one another.
- **Synergy:** the consequential relational dynamic inherent in that interaction; the character and position of its consequences vary.

Synergy is therefore not an optional event that may or may not follow Interaction, and it must not be reduced to a synonym for Interaction. The distinction between the interaction and its consequential dynamic remains analytically necessary.

## 3. Cross-consolidation implications

### 3.1 Interaction ↔ Synergy

The prior conditional model is superseded by the current formulation. Interaction necessarily entails Synergy as an architectural proposition. The research question is no longer whether Synergy exists after an interaction, but how the consequential relational dynamic manifests, what consequences are observed, and how those consequences are characterized.

The formulation's adopted foundational status must be distinguished from empirical validation of universal applicability. “Necessarily entails” is foundational within IFD's current architecture; its universal empirical applicability remains a research proposition.

### 3.2 Synergy ↔ Observation

Observation must investigate consequences across domains and time, including direction, degree, duration, intensity, and temporal movement. These are distinct dimensions. Magnitude, duration, and intensity must not be collapsed into the Generative–Destructive direction.

### 3.3 Synergy ↔ Trace

Trace preserves records of actions, interactions, observed consequences, and subsequent changes. A trace can support later examination, comparison, and temporal reconstruction. A trace does not by itself establish that the interaction caused the recorded consequence, nor does it establish the causal mechanism.

### 3.4 Synergy ↔ collective configurations

Dyadic, multi-identity, and collective arrangements are observational configurations, not definitions of Synergy. More participants, greater complexity, or coordination costs do not predetermine whether consequences are generative or destructive. The relational consequences must be observed.

### 3.5 Earlier candidate mechanisms

Productive surplus, reciprocal BUILD, dyadic APEX, and capacity-ceiling propositions are retained only as candidate patterns, mechanisms, or special cases for investigation. None is a prerequisite or universal definition of Synergy.

## 4. Three open architectural questions — preserve without premature resolution

**A. Is Synergy analytically distinct from Interaction?**

The current formulation requires a distinction between the interaction itself and its consequential relational dynamic. Determine whether the distinction can be specified in operational terms without treating Synergy as a second event or a synonym. Do not assume the answer is settled merely because the terms are distinguished in prose.

**B. Can one interaction have both generative and destructive consequences?**

A single interaction may plausibly improve one domain while damaging another—for example, increasing economic productivity while diminishing trust, autonomy, or individual capacity. If mixed consequences occur, determine whether observations must first be represented across multiple domains or dimensions. An overall one-dimensional spectrum position or aggregate score must not be assigned until its validity and aggregation rules are justified. The published page establishes the two poles and a graduated spectrum but does not yet establish multidimensional measurement or a validated aggregation method.

**C. What is the relationship between Synergy and causality?**

“Consequences arising from interaction” is the adopted foundational description. Instrumentation must nevertheless distinguish:
1. consequences observed after or during interaction;
2. consequences associated with the interaction;
3. consequences for which the interaction is supported as a cause;
4. the causal mechanism, if one is demonstrated.

Temporal sequence, association, or a trace alone is not sufficient to establish causation. Define alternative explanations, comparison conditions, and disconfirming observations appropriate to each research domain.
